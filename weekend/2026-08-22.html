Every significant UK number this week argued for cheaper borrowing, and borrowing got dearer anyway. A softening labour market, inflation exactly on forecast, the best manufacturing orders survey in months and a consumer visibly pulling back — and ten-year PWLB borrowing still ended the week 4bp higher at 6.11%. The deciding factor was somebody else’s debt.
THE WEEK

Monday opened with the previous week’s damage still on the board and the 10-year maturity rate at 6.07%. By Tuesday morning it was 6.15% — the week’s high, and it arrived overnight on nothing domestic at all. That set the pattern: the moves that mattered happened while London slept.

The UK evidence pointed one way all week. Tuesday’s labour market data softened on every measure that matters to the Bank of England. Wednesday’s inflation figure landed exactly on forecast at 2.9%, and the rise came from gas rather than anything persistent. Thursday brought manufacturing orders fifteen points better than expected. Friday brought retail sales that matched forecast on the headline and missed on every measure underneath it — annual growth more than halved, sales excluding fuel down nearly twice as much as expected.

None of it set the price. Ten-year borrowing ended the week 4bp above where it opened. On Thursday the clearest illustration of the whole week: manufacturing orders beat forecast by fifteen points and gilt yields eased in the quarter-hour afterwards. We reported the figure and the price separately, because joining them would have been invention.

What did set the price was the global market in government debt. The German 10-year spent the week at a 15-year high, the US 30-year at levels last seen in 2007, and by Friday’s close US 10-year borrowing costs were reported approaching a 20-month high.

The exception proves it. On Wednesday afternoon the US Treasury announced bond buybacks and the Treasury Secretary intervened publicly to steady the market. It produced the best move of our week — the whole curve 5.6 to 6.4bp lower within a quarter of an hour, and the relief stuck at long maturities, which is where a buyback programme buys. By Thursday’s close the market it started in had given all of it back, and the Treasury Secretary was talking about intervening again: ‘we have a big tool kit’. An intervention that has to be repeated within a day tells you about the strength of what it is pushing against.

Two things built underneath all of it. Energy escalated — Brent above $93, European gas reported doubled with worse expected, US sanctions on Iran widened, and energy is precisely what took UK inflation from 2.6% to 2.9% this month. And the public finances deteriorated, with Friday bringing an unexpected £1.8bn deficit ahead of the new Chancellor’s first Budget — a question about how much the government must borrow, and therefore about long-dated borrowing costs.

WORTH YOUR TIME

Hanno Lustig, ‘America’s Risky Debt: What Markets See That Policymakers Don’t’ — Aspen Institute Economic Strategy Group, published Thursday 20 August 2026. Free to download, no registration.

The argument: for decades global investors paid a premium for the safety and liquidity of US Treasury debt, and that premium has eroded. Lustig sets out three market signals — Treasuries no longer pricing at an advantage to comparable high-grade corporate debt, the old negative correlation between stocks and bonds having reversed so Treasuries no longer behave as a haven in stress, and foreign reserve managers trimming dollar holdings. Policymakers, he argues, still work to a ‘safe-debt’ model; bond investors have stopped.

Why it earns your time this week specifically. On Wednesday we watched the US Treasury intervene to steady its own bond market, and on Thursday we watched the entire effect disappear while the Treasury Secretary talked about doing it again. Lustig’s central warning is precisely about that manoeuvre: that central bank and Treasury intervention risks suppressing the very market signal fiscal policymakers rely on to judge whether the debt is sustainable. He proposes clearer criteria for what counts as market dysfunction, and a Fed–Treasury accord limiting when intervention is allowed.

For a treasurer this is not American trivia. Every rate we reported this week was set by the global market in government debt rather than by anything the UK published, and the single largest move of the week came from a US debt-management decision. This is the clearest recent account of why that market has become so sensitive — and it is a useful counterweight to the assumption, built into a lot of commentary, that an intervention that calms a market has solved something.

We read seven candidates this week. Six did not qualify — four were published outside the week under review, however good (a Bank of England decomposition of last year’s long-rate moves, a NIESR piece on the shortening of gilt issuance, an asset manager’s weekly, and an IPPR analysis), and two could not be read in full without a login. Only work published during the week under review and freely readable goes in, and we never pad.
THE WEEK AHEAD
  • Monday — the week’s first round, and notice 324/26 holds until it. 09:00 brings the German Ifo business survey, forecast 87.2 against 86.6.
  • Wednesday 13:30 — US core PCE price index, the Federal Reserve’s preferred inflation measure, forecast 0.2% on the month against 0.1%. Durable goods orders alongside it, forecast 0.7%.
  • Nothing major on the UK calendar in the first half of the week.

The US core PCE figure is the release most likely to move UK borrowing costs, which is itself the week’s lesson in one line. On a week when the UK published its inflation rate, its labour market and its retail sales, the rate that a British council pays was set in Washington and Berlin.

An empty domestic calendar has not meant a quiet week at any point this month. The two largest moves we reported both arrived overnight, from abroad, on days with nothing scheduled at home.
