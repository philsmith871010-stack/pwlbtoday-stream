Why it exists

Every council writes this report. No two write it the same way.

We read 551 treasury reports — 201 strategies, 133 outturns, 125 mid-year reviews and 92 quarterly monitors — and catalogued every section heading in them. They came to 4,265 distinct headings. Four in five were used by one council and no other.

4,265 headings, as the sector writes them
01

This is not a failure of drafting

Treasury teams are small and the reports are good. What is missing is a shared spine. The Codes say what must be covered, not how to lay it out, so every authority has solved the same problem separately — and solved it well, in its own words.

02

Advisers supply the templates

Which is why councils on the same adviser read alike and councils on different advisers do not. That is a reasonable service, honestly given. But it means the shape of a council's report tells you who advises it, and a member comparing two authorities is comparing two house styles as much as two balance sheets.

03

The benchmark is the point of comparison

The liability benchmark is deliberately built to be compared like for like against a loan portfolio. Presented consistently, it lets a member see their own authority clearly, and lets anyone see how it sits against the rest. Presented differently by every authority that publishes one, it does neither.

What it was built from

A survey, two Codes, a consultation, and the loan book.

The Codes

Read, not summarised

The Prudential Code and the Treasury Management Code, both sets of Guidance Notes, and the 2026 consultation that replaces the single liability benchmark chart with five indicators. Seventy-three binding requirements were extracted and tracked individually: sixty-three are answered, eight fall outside a drafting tool, and two are named as not covered, because a register that only counts successes is a scoreboard rather than a check.

The corpus

Best practice, located rather than assumed

Every section, table and chart in 551 reports was catalogued so the tool's structure follows what the strongest reports actually do — and so we could tell a strategy from an outturn, which turn out to be genuinely different documents rather than the same one in a different tense. A strategy runs to 7,600 words and sets things; an outturn runs to 4,000 and reports what happened. So an outturn or a monitoring report asks for what the period actually cost and earned, against budget and against last year, and a strategy is never asked for it.

The policies

What 143 councils actually permit

Every counterparty policy we could read was extracted from its own published strategy — 1,220 limits across ten sectors, with the duration, the cash cap and the minimum rating each authority sets. So when the tool asks how long you will lend to a money market fund, it can show you the range the sector publishes rather than a number somebody suggested. It is a distribution, not a recommendation.

The data

The loan book, already filled in

All 12,220 PWLB advances to 315 borrowing authorities, run off contractually across fifty years, so the existing-loans line starts populated. It is a starting point you amend, not an answer: it cannot see market debt, LOBOs, or which pool a loan sits in.

How it works

Four passes, and you have a draft.

01

Answer a few questions

Which authority, which report, whether you have an HRA and whether debt is pooled. Twenty-two further choices decide which clauses apply — a council with no HRA is never told about two-pool apportionment; a Scottish authority gets the loans fund and the section 95 officer rather than MRP and section 151; a combined authority gets the section 73 officer. Or turn on a demonstration authority and every one of them is answered for you, so you can read the whole report before typing a figure.

02

Paste your figures

A grid of years down and measures across — CFR, MRP, capital receipts applied, other balance sheet items, your own projection of year-end loans and investments. Paste the block straight out of Excel: it reads your header row, matches it to the columns whatever order they are in and whatever your council calls them, and lines the rows up by financial year. The chart redraws under the grid as you type, so you can see what a change does without leaving the page. For an outturn or a monitoring report you are also asked what the period actually cost and earned — interest paid and income received against the budget for them, the return achieved against its benchmark, and last year's figure beside each.

03

Set your lending policy

Who you will lend to, for how long, and how much — sector by sector, with minimum ratings, country limits, group limits and pooled funds. Every question sits beside what 143 published policies actually say, so you can see where your own limits fall in the range before you set them. It is written into the report as your policy, in tables a committee can read.

04

Take the draft away

LB1 to LB5 drawn and tabled, an authorised limit derived from the projection rather than typed, affordability against the net revenue stream, and every section written in full — with each figure only you can supply left as a visible gap. Wrapped in the committee papers it has to travel in: recommendations, financial and legal implications, and the proper officer for your nation. Export to Word and edit it like any other document.

Housing authorities, and councils without an HRA

One fund or two, and the report knows the difference.

The 2026 proposals treat a housing authority differently at almost every indicator, and differently again depending on how it apportions debt. That is a lot of rules to keep straight by hand, and it is the part of a treasury report that most often goes wrong. Here it follows from one answer on the first screen.

01

No HRA

Eleven rows of figures, and nothing about housing appears anywhere. No second fund, no pooling question, no inter-fund lending series, no separate charts. A council without an HRA is never shown a control it cannot use, and never has to decide whether a paragraph applies to it.

02

One pool

The authority borrows once and apportions. So the HRA is asked for seven rows, not eleven: it has no external loans of its own, and it holds no treasury investments — the General Fund manages liquidity for the whole authority, which is why the consultation says the HRA needs no liquidity allowance. The loans column sits on the General Fund and is marked on the page as covering both.

03

Two pools

Each fund carries its own debt, so LB1, LB2 and LB3 are drawn separately for each, and LB6 shows the position between them. Where the HRA's loans sit below its benchmark the difference is borrowing from the General Fund — but only up to the surplus the General Fund actually has. A fund sitting on its own benchmark has nothing spare to lend, and the chart says so rather than inventing it.

04

Measured against its own income

Affordability is shown for the General Fund, the HRA and the authority as a whole. The HRA's ratio is struck against rents rather than taxpayer and grant income, and for an HRA carrying the debt on its stock it is ordinarily the higher of the two — which is exactly why one blended figure is no use to a housing authority.

LB1 is drawn per fund for every housing authority whatever its pooling approach, and LB3 per fund where there are two pools, because that is what the consultation asks for. The rule is in one place in the code and is checked on every change.

What it is worth to an authority

Weeks, not hours.

A strategy is 8,000 words, sixty figures and a dozen charts, and almost none of the work is writing it. It is re-keying last year's numbers, redrawing what they changed, and then checking that every figure still agrees with every other one. That is the part that takes weeks, and it is the part that is done here before you start.

01

Nothing is missed, and you can show that

Seventy-three binding requirements from the two Codes and their Guidance Notes are tracked one by one. Sixty-three are answered by the tool, eight fall outside what a drafting aid can do, and two are named as not covered because they are your authority's own judgement. A gap you can point to is worth more at audit than a document that looks finished.

What it does not claim

A drafting aid, not a compliance statement.

The tool produces the indicators the Code requires, complete and correctly presented. It does not certify that your authority complies with anything, and it is worth being plain about where the edges are.

  • Nothing is extrapolated. Every year is a figure you entered. A blank year is nil, and the charts show what that implies — which is the honest answer, and what the Guidance Notes require.
  • The loan book is a starting point. It is the published PWLB data and we hold it with confidence, but it cannot see market debt or new borrowing you intend.
  • Two pools cannot be split for you. Nothing published says which pool a loan sits in, so the tool declines to guess and asks for each fund's own projection.
  • Two requirements are not covered. The chief finance officer's personal responsibilities, and the principles for determining prudently investable funds. Both are your authority's own, and both are named in the register rather than quietly dropped.
  • Check every figure before it reaches a committee. It is a draft. It says so on itself.
  • Independent of any adviser. We do not sell treasury advice, arrange loans, or take a position in anything you might do next.
