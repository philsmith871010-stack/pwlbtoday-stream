i The week's best domestic news changed nothing. Britain grew faster than any other G7 economy in the first half of the year, confirmed on Wednesday. Gilt yields rose anyway. When the price of long-dated borrowing is being set by American deficits and the oil price, good UK growth figures do not get a vote.
The 10-year maturity rate ended the week at 6.03%, 5bp above where it closed the week before — but that number hides the week rather than describing it. Borrowing costs were shoved twice by things that happened outside the United Kingdom, and both shoves landed at the long end.
THE WEEK

Ten rounds struck this week, and for most of them nothing much happened. The week was made by two days.

Tuesday was the shock. The morning round, notice 307/26, fixed the 10-year maturity rate at 6.09% — up 10bp on the previous afternoon, and the highest point of the week. The cause was oil: a repricing that pushed inflation expectations up across the curve. It was the single largest one-round move of the week and it happened before most people had read their email.

Wednesday and Thursday gave it back. 309/26 came in at 6.02%, five below Tuesday lunchtime; 310/26 at 6.00%. Thursday sat still at 6.01% for both rounds. By Thursday evening the week looked like a scare that had passed — the 10-year was a single basis point above where it had started on Monday morning.

Then Friday. The United States sold 30-year bonds at 5.216%, the highest cost since around 2001, and a 10-year auction the day before had drawn the steepest financing cost since 2007. That reached London overnight. Friday's rounds fixed at 6.03% both times — modest enough — but after the second round struck at 12:23, two weak American consumer readings landed and gilt yields kept climbing all afternoon. The market finished the day 4.4 to 9.2bp dearer depending on maturity, with none of it reaching a rate anyone could borrow at.

That is the week's shape: the rounds moved 1 to 3bp on Friday while the market moved two to five times as much. Anyone who drew down on Friday got a price set before the day got expensive. Monday inherits the difference.

WORTH YOUR TIME

We read five candidates this week and two earned your time. Both are free, both were published during the week under review, and between them they cover the two ends of the thing that actually drove your borrowing costs.

1. “Treasury Auction Yield Hits Highest in 25 Years”
Committee for a Responsible Federal Budget, 14 August 2026 — crfb.org

Short, and the closest thing to a primary account of Friday's cause. It reports the $25bn 30-year auction clearing at 5.216% and goes past the headline to the demand detail that matters: a bid-to-cover of 2.39 and primary dealers left holding 11.5% of the issue, both weaker than their twelve-month averages, and an awarded yield above the when-issued level — the technical signature of a sale that struggled.

Why it earns your time: this is the event that made your Friday afternoon more expensive, described by people who follow the arithmetic rather than the drama. It is also a useful corrective to reading one auction as a crisis: the piece argues the pressure is structural — a debt path, not a bad day — which is precisely why the effect on the gilt long end did not reverse by the close. Read it for the mechanism, and hold the advocacy at arm's length; the CRFB campaigns for deficit reduction and the conclusion follows the campaign.

2. “Economy slows but doesn’t stall as the UK leads the G7 on growth”
Resolution Foundation, 13 August 2026 — resolutionfoundation.org

The domestic half. UK output grew 0.4% in the second quarter after 0.6% in the first, making Britain the fastest-growing G7 economy in the first half of the year, with 15 of 20 subsectors expanding and information and communications up 2.7% on AI investment. GDP per head rose 0.4%, close to its long-run average — though still 6.6% below the pre-pandemic trend.

Why it earns your time: for the sting in the tail, which is a borrowing story. The Foundation estimates that if the Office for Budget Responsibility marks down its forecasts on the war's energy and supply-chain effects, the hit could absorb around £6bn — roughly a quarter — of the Chancellor's fiscal headroom. Less headroom means more gilt issuance, and more issuance is felt at the long end, which is where councils borrow. It also explains something odd about this week: strong growth figures arrived on Wednesday and did nothing to borrowing costs, because the market is pricing supply and inflation rather than activity.

Three candidates were rejected on date alone — a gilt expected-returns piece from 4 August, a Bank of England decomposition of 2025 long rates from January, and an NIESR note on shortening gilt maturities from March. All three are worth reading; none of them is this week's news, and this section does not pad.

THE WEEK AHEAD

Monday’s round would come in near 6.079% on the 10-year maturity rate (5.879% certainty), 5.579% on the 5-year and 6.639% on the 20-year — 3.9 to 5.9bp above the rounds that struck on Friday. That is a close-anchored estimate of a round that has not struck, with a weekend in front of it.

Three UK releases, all at 07:00, all landing before that morning’s round rather than after it — so unlike most of last week's news, each one can reach a rate the same day:

  • Tuesday — unemployment, forecast 4.9%, unchanged on last.
  • Wednesday — inflation, forecast 3.0% against 2.6% last. The month’s largest scheduled risk to borrowing costs, and on this week’s evidence a surprise would be felt at the long end first.
  • Friday — retail sales, forecast −0.6%.

Globally, the thing most likely to move UK rates is the same one that moved them on Friday: American long-dated supply, and whether the weak consumer readings that closed last week turn into a growth story or stay a deficit story. The oil price is the other watch item, and it has already moved this month’s rates once.
