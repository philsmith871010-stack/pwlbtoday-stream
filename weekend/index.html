The week’s whole move was a change of mind about the Bank of England. Two-year gilt yields ended 4.4bp above the previous Friday while fifty-year yields ended 1.8bp below, and the ten-year finished within a tenth of a basis point of where it began. On Friday afternoon the path itself moved: the rise that follows November’s pulled forward a meeting, from March 2027 to February.

The week

Monday looked like nothing happened. Two rounds were struck — 325/26 at 09:42 and 326/26 at 12:31 — and both left ten-year borrowing at 6.11%, exactly where Friday had left it. Underneath, the gap between two-year and fifty-year yields widened from 0.7bp at the open to 4.4bp at the close. That gap was the week.

Tuesday produced the only round of the week worth waiting for. 327/26 struck at 09:07 with the ten-year at 6.13%, the highest of the ten; by 12:48, 328/26 came in at 6.08%, five basis points better at every maturity and on every repayment type. Gilts closed at the day’s low, about 7bp below Monday.

Wednesday morning was the bottom. 329/26 struck at 09:12 at 6.04% — the lowest of the week at every single maturity. Then the market turned inside the same session: gilts closed at the day’s high, 4 to 5bp above Tuesday, and by 12:27 the lunchtime round had already given a basis point back.

From that point the direction never changed. Thursday added two basis points at each round on an empty calendar, the whole day’s movement fitting inside four. Friday added two more, and then the afternoon brought the repricing that explains the week: the Fed chair said inflation is not slowing, and between two o’clock and half past three the market pulled a Bank Rate rise forward by a meeting.

Six consecutive rounds, 329/26 through 334/26, each one above the one before: 6.04, 6.05, 6.07, 6.09, 6.10, 6.12.

Rate expectations. The one-year SONIA swap — the clearest read on what markets expect the Bank of England to do over the coming year — finished at 4.182% against 4.142% the previous Friday, four basis points higher. Bank Rate itself is unchanged at 3.75%, and the first rise is still priced for November. What changed this week is the one after it, which moved forward from March 2027 to February.

Worth your time

Stephen Hunsaker and Simon Pittaway, ‘The Macroeconomic Policy Outlook: Q3 2026’ — Resolution Foundation, published Monday 24 August 2026. Free to download, no registration. resolutionfoundation.org

The argument. The headline productivity figures say the UK is going nowhere. The authors go to administrative tax records instead and find output per hour growing 1.1% a year over the two years to Q2 2026 — against 0.7% in the late 2010s, and against a fall of 0.7% a year in the period before. They then work to rule out the flattering explanations. The improvement is broad, showing up in 12 of 19 sectors. It is within sectors rather than a shift between them, so it is not the economy reshuffling workers into better-paid work. Investment has been flat at 19% of GDP since 2023, so it is not more machinery per worker. What is left is genuine efficiency gain, and they are careful to say that artificial intelligence cannot yet be the cause: 46% of firms report using it, but only 4.6% use it extensively.

Why it earns your time this week specifically. On Friday afternoon the market decided the Bank of England will have to move sooner, and that decision is what put three basis points on two-year borrowing over the week. Underneath any such decision is a view about how fast the economy can grow before it generates inflation. This paper argues that capacity is being measured too pessimistically — and it was published on the Monday of the very week the curve moved the other way.

It matters twice over for a treasurer. If the reading holds, the case for rate rises priced this week is weaker than the curve says. And productivity is the single largest lever in the fiscal forecast: the OBR currently assumes about 1% in the medium term, which is below what this data shows. That assumption drives the borrowing forecast, the borrowing forecast drives gilt supply, and gilt supply is what these rates are built on.

Eight candidates were read this week. Six were published outside the week under review, and one — a term premium tracker that would otherwise have fitted the week exactly — is behind a members-only wall and dated July.

The week ahead

Monday is the summer bank holiday. No fixing, and no gilt market. Notice 334/26 holds from Friday lunchtime through to Tuesday morning — a single price standing for four days, which is longer than any rate has stood this month.

Tuesday 1 September brings the week’s first round, shortly after nine. On Friday’s close it reads a little above 334/26 at the shorter maturities and a little below at the longest. Three days of overseas trading sit in front of it.

The euro-area inflation flash lands at one o’clock on Monday, forecast 2.9% against 2.8%, with no UK market open to take it. Whatever it says arrives in Tuesday’s opening prices instead, an hour or so before the round strikes. China’s manufacturing survey (forecast 49.7, from 49.2) and Japanese consumer confidence both land in the small hours of Monday.

Energy is the open question. The weekend has brought a large new American oil supply agreement and, at the same time, an intensifying conflict and widening sanctions around Iran. Those pull in opposite directions on the price of energy, and energy has been behind more of this month’s moves in UK borrowing costs than the UK calendar has.

Which is the week’s lesson repeating itself. Nothing on the UK calendar sits in the first half of next week, and the week just gone had a nearly empty domestic calendar too — and still moved ten-year borrowing through a nine basis point range and ended with the Bank Rate path redrawn.
