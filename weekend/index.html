A week that ran up the stairs and came back down the escalator. PWLB borrowing costs climbed steadily into Friday morning — the 10-year standard rate touched 6.00% at the AM fixing, its highest of the run — before a shock US jobs report (23,000 jobs lost against 80,000 expected) pulled market rates back and left Monday's estimated fixing almost exactly where Friday's ended: 10-year at 5.975%. Net on the week, the 10-year standard rate is about 4bp higher. This is the first WEEKEND READ — the week's story in full, the numbers that matter, and two pieces of research worth your time with a cup of coffee.

The week — up the stairs, down the escalator

The watch joined mid-week, so the story starts Thursday: gilt yields rose 4–6bp on the day and PWLB rates followed at Friday morning's fixing — up 4–6bp across the board, the 10-year standard rate printing 6.00% and the 20-year 6.56%. The push had global fingerprints: energy-supply nerves around the Strait of Hormuz, a China export beat, US chip-tariff noise, and a US Federal Reserve whose last meeting produced three dissents in favour of an immediate rate rise.

Friday told the other half of the story. The lunchtime fixing eased 1–2bp (10-year to 5.98%), and at 13:30 UK the US reported losing 23,000 jobs against an expected 80,000 gain — the kind of miss that changes minds. Market rates fell sharply, UK expectations followed, and although the move faded through the afternoon (markets gave back roughly two-thirds of it), the week closed with gilt yields 0.9–1.9bp lower on the day and the rise the market prices from the Bank of England pushed a little further out.

Where that leaves the Bank of England picture: Bank Rate stays 3.75% now; September is priced as a hold; the first rise is priced for November; and the second hovers around the turn of Q1 2027 — its timing visibly finely balanced, flickering between March and April as Friday's data landed. For borrowing decisions: Monday's estimated fixings sit 0.3–1.1bp below Friday's — essentially level — so nothing in the data argues for urgency either way; the weekend's Hormuz and Ukraine headlines are the wildcard that could reset Monday's open.

Worth your time — two reads
Bank of England · Bank Insights · 17 July 2026 · ~8 min
Bank Rate expectations in the UK curve following the war in Iran

Two Bank staff economists, Andras Lengyel and Danny Walker, examine the same market curve our “what markets expect the Bank of England to do” panel is built from — and argue it currently overstates the chance of rate rises. Stripping out risk premium with a term-structure model, they find the model-implied Bank Rate path is “broadly flat over the next year”, in line with what surveyed market participants actually say they expect. The upward slope, on this reading, is mostly compensation for uncertainty — at levels unusual outside 2022–23 — rather than a genuine forecast of hikes.

Why it earns your time: our brief has reported a first rise priced for November all week. This is the strongest public argument that pricing like that can flatter the true odds — which cuts both ways for a borrower: less reason to rush a drawdown to “beat” November, but also a reminder that curve-implied paths can deflate quickly, exactly as Friday afternoon showed.

NIESR · David Aikman · 9 January 2026 · ~11 min
Could a gilt market shock derail the economy in 2026?

Written in January, when the consensus saw gilt yields drifting down toward 4.4% — a central case the year has comprehensively overtaken. Read it instead for the risk half, which has aged remarkably well: a step-by-step map of how a yield repricing becomes a market spiral — leveraged trades unwinding, repo funding tightening, redemptions from the non-bank funds that now dominate gilt-market liquidity — and why the Bank of England faces a genuine dilemma about intervening without becoming a standing backstop.

Why it earns your time: the January “risk scenario” is a fair description of the world since February's war. For a treasurer, its value is the transmission map — what a bad fortnight in gilts does to PWLB rates, how fast, and why “wait for it to blow over” has not always been a safe strategy in this market structure. Note the date throughout: it is a framework piece, not a current forecast.

Selection standard: freely accessible at time of writing, read in full before inclusion, publication date always shown. Sources this week were checked against fresher weekly commentary (BlackRock Investment Institute and peers); these two won on substance.
